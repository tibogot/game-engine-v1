Track builder manual

Building a jump

Lay your run-up, place a ramp from the Ramps tab, and the chain's open end is now the lip. From there you have two ways to cross the gap, and they are not interchangeable.

The car flies to a separate landing — the usual case. Open the dev panel's Gap / jump section, set Launch speed to the speed you expect the car to arrive at and Landing drop to how far below the lip you want it to come down, then press Snap landing → new chain. That solves the real trajectory and starts a new chain exactly where the car lands, heading down-arc — no grid rounding, because that position is physics, not authoring. Put a Landing ramp (L) or a Platform there to catch it.

You fly straight over a hole and land back on the same line — that is the Gap piece (G, Ramps tab). It builds no road at all: it just advances the chain by its length and drops it, leaving air in between. Sizes are on the tiles (Gap 20 / 40 / 60 m, and two with drops), but the honest way is Size the Gap piece to this jump in the Gap / jump panel — it measures the same trajectory and sets the length and drop to match, so the hole is one the car can actually clear. It declines if the jump lands off to one side, because a gap can only run straight on.

Both buttons solve the arc when you press them, so they work whether or not Arc preview is ticked. And size the landing generously: the car will not always arrive at exactly your launch speed, so a long platform beats a tight ramp.

How it works

Props & movers: pick one above and a ghost follows your mouse — LMB to place (keeps going, so you can lay a run of cones), Esc or RMB to put it down. The Snap button picks the surface it rides: Ground = terrain, Road = decks only (ghost turns red off the road), Auto = whichever you are pointing at. Placed something already? Right-click it to select and fine-tune with the gizmo.

Road pieces — point at the track. The next piece follows your mouse, just like a prop ghost: move the cursor near any open end and the piece jumps there, switching chains for you. LMB/Enter places it. Point at nothing and the piece stays exactly where it is, so a piece you positioned by hand is never dragged off by a stray mouse move.
The floating arrows are the open ends, and each points the way a piece placed there would grow: blue = a chain's end (keep going), purple = a chain's start (builds backwards), gold = a junction exit (starts a side road). The big white one is where the piece will actually land if you click now.

The gizmo is for empty space. Use it when you want a piece somewhere there is no track yet — drag to move it anywhere (Y axis = height), and it still magnets onto an open end if you drop it close enough. W move Shift+E rotate • Q/E yaw nudge • N new chain • [/] select chain (gizmo then drags the whole chain).
After a jump, press X for LOCAL axes. World axes line up with the build grid, which is the right frame for dropping a chain somewhere tidy — but a takeoff points wherever your last corner left it, so nudging a landing pad along the flight line becomes a mix of two drags with no way to hold the line. In local axes the blue arrow is the direction of travel, red is sideways and green is up: one drag, one axis. Grid snap follows the choice — world rounds each axis onto a cell, local rounds the distance you moved.

Selecting a section. Right-click a piece, then Shift+right-click another in the same chain and everything between them is selected — one gesture for "this stretch". Ctrl+right-click adds or removes a single piece. The brighter highlight is the anchor, the end a range measures from, so shift-clicking again re-measures from there.
Everything then acts on the whole run, as ONE undo step: Del delete, Enter replace with the palette piece, L level, U guardrails on/off, and the arrows bank the section.
Banking a section is not the same as tilting each piece in it. A tilt is applied at a piece's seam and carried down the chain, so writing 15° onto five pieces would give you 15, 30, 45, 60, 75° — a corkscrew. Instead the angle goes on once at the front and comes off again at the back: a constant bank through the section, level track past it. Roll in, roll out.

Exact angles — use the arrow keys. Dragging the rotate gizmo is fine for eyeballing, hopeless for repeating. The arrows turn whatever is selected by exactly one Angle step (the dev panel's Grid Snap setting, 15° by default), so three presses is 45° every time and pressing back returns you to precisely zero: ←→ yaw, ↑↓ pitch (↑ is nose up), Shift+←→ roll. The status line shows the angle you are now at. Q/E use the same step.
The axes are the piece's, not the world's — roll is about the direction of travel, so banking a corner never changes where it points. Which thing turns depends on what you have: a piece you right-clicked tilts (and banks everything after it in the chain — that is the banked-landing-strip tool; L levels it again), a chain you picked with [/] turns rigidly, and the next-piece ghost takes yaw only, because every socket in the kit is level by convention.

Either end: a chain grows from its tail or its head — press O (or drag the ghost onto the far end) to switch, and the status line always says which one you are on. Building backwards prepends: the piece goes in front and the chain's start moves back to meet it, so nothing already placed moves — you can extend a finished run backwards to a new start line without breaking a single seam you had aligned. A start that is already joined to a junction branch or to another chain is not an open end, so O will decline there and say so.

Junctions — one arm continues by itself. A fork, T, crossroads or roundabout is an ordinary chain piece: the chain drives in one end and out the far side, so you do not have to do anything to keep going on the main arm — just place the next piece.
The EXTRA exits are the floating yellow arrows. Point at one (or press K) and place, and the side road starts as its own chain; the arrow disappears once something is built on it. A crossroads and a roundabout have two arrows — K steps through them one press at a time, and pointing at one goes straight to it. R flips which side a Split / T uses and which arm of a Y fork the chain follows. A roundabout's island is a hole — cut across the middle and you drop through it.

Splitting and rejoining: build the alternate route off a Split's arrow, run it where you like, then press J (Join) to close the gap back into a Merge. A chain is a rigid run of pieces, so nothing you place by hand will ever land on both ends at once — J generates a link shaped to whatever is left over, meeting the far end exactly in position and heading. It tells you the gap it closed and the tightest radius, and refuses outright if the only curve that would fit is a hairpin — build a little further out and try again.

Undo: Backspace or Ctrl+Z steps back through every edit — placing, deleting, replacing, tilting, dragging, even Clear. Shift+Backspace, Ctrl+Y or Ctrl+Shift+Z redoes; right-click the Undo button for the same. The shortcuts follow the letter PRINTED on your key, so Ctrl+Z is Ctrl+Z on AZERTY too — and Backspace works on any layout at all. A gizmo drag is one step, not one per frame; loading a track starts a fresh history.
